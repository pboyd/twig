To do lists are personal things and I've always felt constrained by the one-size-fits-all apps I've used. That's why I started Twig and I selfishly designed it to be "one size that fits me", but I don't think my needs are really so special, which is why I'm publishing it. This document is my own philosophy of task management, which I've tried to encode in Twig. If this resonates with you, you might like Twig. If not, well, at least you know.

More often than I care to admit, I feel like I'm on a treadmill that's set a little too fast. My house is always getting dirty, something is always breaking, my car needs maintenance, and my kids want dinner. My job imposes a completely separate list of responsibilities. And, oh yeah, there's stuff I actually want to do, none of which stands any chance of getting done if I don't plan it. It can be frantic and overwhelming, but letting something slip is unacceptable, both socially and professionally. I only know of two strategies for this problem: *slow down* or *speed up*.

## Slowing down

Here's a current sample from my personal to do list:

* Trim tree limbs in the backyard
* Clean the microwave
* Take broken chair to the dump
* Drop off items to donate at the thrift store
* Fix [child]'s broken pinewood derby trophy
* Write a philosophy of Twig document

If I'm serious about slowing down, then I need to know which of those I can skip. Or, better yet, avoid in the first place. So why do I need to do those things? Let's take them one by one:

* *Trim tree limbs in the backyard*: the low branches on the pine trees make it hard to mow the grass around them
* *Clean the microwave*: it's disgusting and we eat food from there
* *Take broken chair to the dump*: I'm not able to fix it, and it's been useless clutter long enough
* *Drop off items to donate at the thrift store*: things that the kids have outgrown, and some clothing we don't wear anymore; someone may be able to use them
* *Fix [child]'s broken pinewood derby trophy*: it's worth keeping, but presently broken
* *Write a philosophy of Twig document*: honestly, I'm second-guessing the necessity

Must we mow the lawn? I understand lawns began as status symbols for 17th-18th-century European aristocrats. How did they come to dominate the 21st-century summers of this middle-class American? I'll probably never understand. Likewise, microwaves were meant to save time on cooking, and I suppose they do, but it's also one more thing to clean and maintain. Did we need that chair, those clothes, and toys in the first place? Probably not.

Most of the stuff I have to do could have been avoided. If I could do less, then I could hold it all in my head and I wouldn't need a to do list. This is, more or less, _Epicureanism_. In short, enjoy life by doing less and let go of all the stress that comes from trying to do it all. Recognize that the stuff you have, the stuff you want, the things you're doing, and things you want to do don't matter and won't make you happy anyway. Lead a quiet and simple life and try to be happy.

That sounds nice, in principle. I once imagined myself enjoying a slower pace of life, so I started a vegetable garden. There's nothing wrong with a garden, but if you start one to find serenity and imagine yourself like Henry David Thoreau growing peas by Walden Pond, then I think you'll find as I did that you have a lot of new stuff to buy, maintain and store, and so many more chores that your life only becomes more frantic. A few years on, I have one overgrown garden bed remaining and a mound next to it resembling nothing so much as an oversized grave containing the bones of my simpler life. Apparently, I can only conceive of simplifying my life in terms of adding things and processes to it, which is no simplification at all.

Twig is absolutely not for people looking to slow down their lives. If you want to slow down, then I applaud you, but as I'm incapable of taking it easy, I'm incapable of building software to help you take it easy, so look elsewhere.

## Speeding up

If we can't slow down, then we need to find a way to organize all the stuff we have to do so we can stay on top of things. It does this in two ways: 1) helping you remember what to do, and 2) focusing your attention to what matters.

I will acknowledge the arrogance embedded in this argument. Somehow we believe we're capable of greater things, and that by declaring our intentions we can will things into existence. Twig will allow you to double down on the folly and declare how long each item will take and when you will do it. _Man plans, God laughs._ I mentioned earlier that I had a task to fix a broken trophy. It's badly damaged plastic, and my first attempt failed because I couldn't make it stand up long enough for the glue to set. I set it aside for a while, devised a strategy involving a copious amount of packing tape to hold it in place temporarily, and went for another round. So much of my life is contingent on things I can't control. We're always one bad step from a concussion, and one phone call away from planning a funeral. In this case, it was nothing so dramatic: the kid who left his trophy on the floor, where it was stepped on, also lost the piece we needed to re-attach. I have no idea where it is, and so I must accept fate.

So, yes, Twig lets you make plans, and, yes, God may laugh at them, but that doesn't mean they're completely useless. I don't consider a plan to be some sort of "commitment to yourself". There are plenty of days where my 7 AM declaration of intent meets my 11 AM emergency and my 3 PM lack of will. Plans help me make the best use of the time I do have, and I've tried to avoid adding anything in Twig that would make me feel guilty about the rest. For instance, I previously used Todoist, where in order to choose what I wanted to do on a particular day I had to assign a due date. If, for whatever reason, the task wasn't completed that day, it turned red and I had to update the due dates to the next day. That's exactly the kind of demotivating workflow I'm trying to avoid. If you don't finish the stuff on today's plan, tomorrow you make a new plan.

I've also avoided gamification for similar reasons. Sometimes I could use more motivation, but, more often than not, missing my "daily target" and messing up my "streak" are merely extra stress on days I simply can't do it.

## Tasks

Tasks are the core of Twig, they're meant to be things you need to do. Twig doesn't care how you write your tasks, but I personally find that tasks work best if they're something I can do _right now_ (assuming I have the time and the inclination):

- Make a doctor's appointment
- Clean the bathroom
- Take the car to get inspected

My personal rules are that a task should not be open ended, and it shouldn't be so enormous that I need to chip away at it over several days. Ideally, I can do it in one session. David Allen, in _Getting Things Done_, used cleaning out a garage as an example. Putting "clean out the garage" sounds like a task, but if your garage looks like mine (or, I suppose, David Allen's), then it's a nebulous and monumental task that will sit on your to do list until you miraculously have a full week with nothing better to do. To do lists are meant to help you organize your life, but if your list is full of "learn French", "buy a house", and "travel to Hawaii", then it will be demoralizing instead of empowering. Pick one aspect of that monster you can actually do (e.g. "sort kid's riding toys", "look for a French class").

Of course, I say this, but my mind always churns up things that are too big to make a good task, and that's why Twig has sub-tasks. I need to get the big thing written down and out of my head sometimes, and then I can break it down later into manageable chunks:

- [ ] Clean out the garage
    - [ ] Drop off donation items
        - [ ] Sort kid's riding toys
        - [ ] Sort yard tools
    - [ ] List broken lawnmower on Craigslist for free
        - [ ] Photograph old lawnmower

I can't clean out the garage this evening, but I can probably find time to photograph a broken lawnmower after work. The thing gets done, a little bit at a time.

I find it frustrating to see things on my task list that I can't actually do. I can't always avoid it, but a frequent issue is something that I can't do _yet_. Twig allows a task to be snoozed for this reason. For instance, my car is due for its state inspection, but it's not urgent enough to take time off from work. So I have snoozed the task until the weekend when I can actually do something about it.

## Daily plans

A list of stuff to do is great, but there will always be more to do than can be done in any given day. This is where daily plans come in. Tasks are things that _could_ be done today, whereas a daily plan is what I think I'm able to do. Plans in Twig have three items:

- **Scheduled tasks**: stuff to do at a specific time
- **Unscheduled tasks**: stuff to do whenever
- **Events**: stuff that happens at a specific time (meetings, appointments, etc.)

I don't use this much at home, but it's great for work. At the beginning of the work day, I'll populate the plan with any meetings I have, set aside some time for lunch, or anything else that occurs at a fixed time. Then I review my tasks and see which ones I need to try and get done that day. I'll estimate the time that the meatier tasks will take and slot them into one of the gaps between the fixed events. When I'm out of available slots, I have to make some hard decisions about what's not getting done today.

At one time, I attempted to lay out my day on paper this way. I could sometimes make it until 9 AM before I needed to scratch something out. Twig lets you re-arrange your day after the fact as much as you want without any guilt. I don't view my daily plans as a strong commitment, and I hope you don't either. It's a tool to let you be realistic about how much you can expect to do in a day, and it also helps you see what you wanted to be doing at any particular moment.

## Goals

I said earlier that a task should ideally be something you can do at one time, or be broken down into manageable pieces of work. But what about bigger things that can't easily be broken up like that? For instance, I said "Learn French" is a terrible task, and it is, but what do you do if you really do want to learn French? You can break it down into smaller steps (e.g. "find a French class", "look for introductory French books", etc.), but learning a language isn't something you're likely to be able to plan out like that. In Twig, "Learn French" should be a _goal_. You can attach tasks to it (e.g. "finish this week's French-class homework"). Goals also have a log of status updates, which you can use to record your progress.

For a concrete example, I want to replace the shelf above the washer and dryer with cabinets. It's a bit more involved than a task, so I made a goal for it. I've never hung a cabinet before, so I made a task to research if it was feasible for a DIY project and get a ballpark estimate for the budget. A helpful guy on YouTube made it sound possible (though it will probably take me a full weekend), and I'll probably need $600. It's not urgent enough to put it on a credit card, or pilfer money set aside for some other purpose, so it's currently on hold until I've saved the money. Once the funds are available, I'll add tasks to schedule a weekend and buy supplies, etc.

Goals are also meant to help with a pernicious effect of to do lists where it feels as good to complete a meaningless task as a meaningful one. If I'm not careful, I can fill my workday with urgent, but unimportant items (e.g. almost every Slack message), or busy-work (e.g. "organize inbox"), while ignoring an important project with a tight deadline. Making a list of goals and reviewing it regularly helps me stay on track.

Goals in Twig have a state. Sometimes a goal is just a random idea I have one day, like maybe I should take a vacation to Hawaii. Goals begin in the **Incubating** state. When I remember that I don't like the beach, long plane rides, or spending money, the "Vacation in Hawaii" goal moves to the **Archived** state. I find a lot of value from considering something seriously and then deciding not to pursue it. It's not a failure, but a conscious decision to focus limited resources somewhere else instead. Now when my wife informs me that long plane rides are fine, and that I'd better get used to the coexisting feelings of sand in my shorts and the weight of a lighter wallet, then the goal becomes **In Progress**. Perhaps one day when the final suitcase has been put away and my exhausted, jet-lagged self collapses in bed, I can pull out my phone and mark that goal **Done**.
